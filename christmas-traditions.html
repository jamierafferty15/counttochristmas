Why we do the things we do

Christmas Traditions

Christmas traditions feel timeless, but most are a mixture of older winter customs, religious observance, family habits and surprisingly recent inventions. Their meaning changes from place to place — and sometimes from one household to the next.

The Christmas tree

Decorated evergreen trees became especially associated with Christmas in German-speaking parts of Europe before spreading more widely. In Britain, the custom grew dramatically in the nineteenth century. The appeal is easy to understand: an evergreen tree brings colour, light and life indoors during the darkest part of winter.

Stockings and small gifts

The stocking tradition is linked to stories about Saint Nicholas giving gifts secretly. In modern homes the stocking has become its own ritual: usually smaller, sillier and more immediate than the presents under the tree.

Crackers and paper hats

Christmas crackers are particularly familiar in Britain and Ireland. Pulling a cracker is part game, part table decoration and part excuse to wear a flimsy paper crown during dinner. The jokes are expected to be bad; arguably that is part of the tradition.

Mince pies and Christmas pudding

Both foods changed over centuries. Mince pies became sweeter as meat disappeared from most recipes, while Christmas pudding developed into the dense fruit-and-spice dessert associated with British Christmas dinners. Many families still make or buy pudding well before Christmas Day.

Boxing Day

26 December is Boxing Day in the UK and several Commonwealth countries. The exact origin of the name is debated, but it has long been associated with giving, service and boxes of gifts or money. Today it is also a major day for sport, shopping and leftovers.

Family traditions matter too

Not every meaningful tradition has centuries of history. A particular film on Christmas Eve, the same breakfast every year, a walk after dinner or one ornament with a family story can become just as important. Traditions gain value through repetition.
