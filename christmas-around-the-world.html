Christmas Around the World

A small introduction to different festive rhythms, from December gatherings to January celebrations.

Updated 7 November 2026

One season, many ways to celebrate

A countdown to 25 December is a familiar marker, but it cannot represent every Christmas celebration. Families may gather on Christmas Eve, keep a religious calendar with a different Christmas date, or continue festive customs into January. Some people celebrate mainly as a religious occasion, some as a cultural holiday, and some do not celebrate it.

The examples below are starting points, not descriptions of every person in a country. Regional traditions, migration, faith and individual choices all shape what happens in a home. Ask someone about their own celebration before assuming a national custom is part of it.

Germany: anticipation through Advent

Christmas markets, lights and Advent customs are prominent parts of the run-up to Christmas in Germany. They make the weeks beforehand a season with its own rituals, rather than merely preparation for one day. Deutschland.de’s Christmas overview introduces this festive setting.

For a simple idea inspired by that rhythm, set aside a weekly moment for a warm drink and a shared activity. You do not need to reproduce a market at home. The useful lesson is to give the waiting period a few recognisable pauses instead of saving all the enjoyment for Christmas Day.

Sweden: Lucia on 13 December

Lucia brings singing processions into the Swedish festive season on 13 December. Visit Sweden’s Lucia guide describes the celebration and its associations. It offers a different reference point within December, with light and music taking centre stage.

Read about the tradition in its own context before borrowing its appearance for an activity. Listening to a Lucia song and finding Sweden on a map can be a thoughtful introduction for children. Discuss how a celebration can make a dark time of year feel communal without suggesting that all households observe it identically.

Italy: the season continues to Epiphany

The Befana is associated with Epiphany, 6 January, in Italian tradition. Italia.it explains the Befana and Epiphany. This gives the festive calendar a later point of anticipation beyond Christmas Day and New Year.

If you are comparing customs as a family, draw a timeline from early December to early January. Mark Christmas Eve, Christmas Day and Epiphany separately. It is easier to understand different gift-giving stories when they are connected with their own dates, rather than treated as interchangeable versions of the same morning.

Britain: traditions around the table

Crackers and mince pies are familiar parts of many British Christmas gatherings. Crackers have a history connected with confectioner Tom Smith, while earlier mince pies included meat. Read the V&A’s cracker history and English Heritage’s food history for the stories behind them.

These examples show how objects and recipes carry history into ordinary homes. A paper hat or a familiar baking smell can become a reminder of past gatherings. They also change: families adapt menus, replace gifts inside crackers, or create their own customs around the meal.

The same date, a different season

December belongs to different seasons in the Northern and Southern Hemispheres. A snowy Christmas illustration is therefore a setting for this website, rather than a description of Christmas weather everywhere. In the Southern Hemisphere, 25 December falls during summer. Actual conditions still depend on location and the weather that year.

When making cards or planning a school activity, try imagining the celebration without snow: what colours, food or outdoor spaces would you choose? This helps separate the meaning of the occasion from the winter imagery used in many books and films.

Explore with curiosity

Choose one place at a time, read a source from that place, and notice which date or activity matters in the account. If someone shares a personal memory, let their story stand as their own rather than using it to speak for an entire country.

Our timer has one clear destination: local midnight at the start of 25 December. The wider season is richer and more varied. Return to the live countdown or explore the traditions guide for ways to create a celebration that fits your own household.
